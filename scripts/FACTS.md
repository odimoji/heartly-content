# Research we may cite

Odi (15 Nov 2026): end videos with a bit more education, not a question. Say that writing it out is shown to help, with real research. These are the only studies to cite until more are added here with a source. Use the wording given (or weaker). Never round up, never add numbers that are not here, never say "proven" or "cures", and never make a health or treatment claim.

## 1. Naming a feeling calms the brain's alarm
- Study: Lieberman and colleagues, UCLA, Psychological Science, 2007. Brain scans of 30 people.
- Finding: when people put a feeling into words, the amygdala (the brain's alarm centre) responded less.
- Say: "In a UCLA brain-scan study, putting a feeling into words turned down the brain's alarm response."
- Do not say: that it was about writing or journaling (people labelled emotions they saw), or give a percentage.
- Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/

## 2. Writing worries down frees up your head
- Study: Ramirez and Beilock, University of Chicago, Science, 2011.
- Finding: students who spent 10 minutes writing about their worries right before an exam did better. The most anxious students improved by nearly one grade point (for example B-minus to B-plus).
- Say: "Chicago researchers had anxious students write out their worries for ten minutes before an exam. They scored nearly a grade higher."
- Do not say: that it works for everyone (only the anxious students benefited), or that it is about heartbreak.
- Source: https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/01/110113141605.htm

## 3. Writing about what weighs on you helps, modestly
- Study: Frattaroli, Psychological Bulletin, 2006. A review of 146 randomised studies of expressive writing (the method James Pennebaker started in 1986).
- Finding: a small but real overall benefit for psychological and physical health. Effects vary a lot between people and studies.
- Say: "Across 146 studies, writing about what's weighing on you had a small but real benefit."
- Do not say: "proven to make you happier", or any size beyond "small but real".
- Source: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3830620

## How to use one
- One fact per video, spoken by the narrator just before or just after the Heartly line, with a short on-screen label (for example "UCLA · 2007").
- Pick the fact that matches the story: 1 for feelings you can't name, 2 for worry and 3am loops, 3 for anything carried a long time.
- Heartly is the next step, never the thing the study tested. Do not imply the research was about Heartly or about AI.

## 4. Writing tomorrow's list down helps you fall asleep (added 15 Nov, found by the content chat, checked here)
- Study: Scullin and colleagues, Baylor University, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2018. 57 healthy adults aged 18 to 30, one night in a sleep lab.
- Finding: people who spent five minutes writing a to-do list for the next few days fell asleep about nine minutes faster than people who wrote about tasks they had already finished.
- Say: "In a sleep lab, people who wrote down tomorrow's to-do list before bed fell asleep nine minutes faster."
- Do not say: that it was journaling or writing about feelings, or that it treats insomnia (the authors say they don't know if it holds for insomnia).
- Source: https://news.web.baylor.edu/news/story/2018/can-writing-your-dos-help-you-doze-baylor-study-suggests-jotting-down-tasks-can
